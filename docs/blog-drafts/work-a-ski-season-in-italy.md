If you hold an EU, EEA or Swiss passport, yes — you can take a job in Italy without a visa at all. If you do not, Italy is harder than Canada or Australia, and for one specific reason: there is no general working holiday scheme, only bilateral agreements with a short list of countries, and the default version of that visa caps you at **three months with the same employer** — which is shorter than a ski season.

That one rule decides whether an Italian season is possible for you, so it is worth settling before you do anything else.

## Who can work in Italy without a visa?

Citizens of the EU, the EEA (Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway) and Switzerland can live and work in Italy under free movement. No visa, no work permit, no quota — you register locally once you are there. The official summary of what that involves is on the European Commission's [Your Europe](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/work/work-abroad/index_en.htm) pages.

Everyone else needs a visa, and the type matters more than the paperwork.

## The working holiday route, and the rule that catches seasonal workers

Italy does not run a single global working holiday programme. It runs separate bilateral agreements, and the Italian Ministry of Labour publishes [the list of countries they cover](https://www.lavoro.gov.it/temi-e-priorita/immigrazione/focus-on/Cooperazione-internazionale/Pagine/Accordi-vacanze-lavoro.aspx). As that page stands, the agreements in force are with New Zealand, Canada, Australia, Hong Kong, South Korea and Japan. If your passport is not on that list, this route is not open to you and you are looking at a sponsored work visa instead.

The part nobody mentions is how much work the visa actually allows:

| | What the official sources say |
|---|---|
| **The general rule** | Under Italy's immigration decree, a working holiday permits up to **six months of work in a year**, and **no more than three months with the same employer**. |
| **South Korea and Japan** | The ministry names these two agreements as exceptions: six months of work, *including* with the same employer. |
| **Canada** | The Italian consulate in Toronto states that Canadian participants may work for the entire authorised period of stay, with the same employer, up to 12 months — and may take part twice. |
| **Australia and New Zealand** | Not something we can tell you. The general rule above may or may not be modified by your agreement, and we are not going to guess on your behalf. |

**A ski season in the Alps runs longer than three months.** So if you are on an agreement where the three-month cap applies, a full season on one contract is not something the visa supports, and an employer offering you one either does not know that or is not worried about it. Neither is a good position for you to be in.

This is the question to put to the Italian embassy or consulate that covers where you live — in writing, before you book a flight. The Foreign Ministry keeps [the directory of embassies and consulates](https://www.esteri.it/en/ministero/struttura/ambasciate-consolati/), and the official visa portal is [vistoperitalia.esteri.it](https://vistoperitalia.esteri.it/). Conditions, quotas and fees change on government timetables, which is exactly why they are not written into this page.

## Common questions

### Do you need a visa to work a ski season in Italy?

Only if you are not an EU, EEA or Swiss citizen. Those passports carry the right to work in Italy with no visa. Everyone else needs one, and for seasonal resort work the realistic options are a working holiday visa, if your country has an agreement with Italy, or a sponsored work visa through the employer.

### Can Australians work a ski season in Italy?

Australia is one of the countries with a working holiday agreement with Italy, so the visa route exists. What we cannot tell you is whether your agreement allows a full season with one employer, or whether the general three-month-per-employer limit applies. Ask the Italian consulate covering your state, in writing, before you commit to anything.

### Is three months long enough for a ski season?

No. An Alpine season runs from roughly December to April, so a three-month cap on working for one employer does not cover it. If that limit applies to your visa, you are looking at a shorter stint or two separate employers, and you should say so to an employer up front rather than discover it mid-season.

### Do you need to speak Italian to work in an Italian resort?

For most of the jobs we see, yes — at least enough to work in. Guest-facing roles in the Dolomites can also want German, because parts of South Tyrol are German and Ladin speaking rather than Italian speaking. Only a handful of the adverts on our board ask for English.
